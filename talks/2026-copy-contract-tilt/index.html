Copy, Contract, Tilt

Tracing the em dash through pretraining, SFT and DPO

Tommie Juzek

Friday 13 November 2026 · 1:20 pm
Love 106, Florida State University

CopyPretraining: the model writes the dash in proportion to what it read.

ContractSFT: the differences pretraining left shrink, to about a quarter on news.

TiltDPO: what is there is multiplied, by the contrast between preferred and rejected answers.

About the talk

Language models have house styles, and some of their habits travel: words associated with AI now turn up in unscripted human speech, and AI help can move what an essay argues, not just how it reads. Where do such habits come from?

This talk treats one habit, the em dash, as a tracer dye. We train small language models from scratch, dose the dash at each stage of training (pretraining, supervised fine-tuning and preference optimisation, in every combination) and read where it comes out. Three verbs sum up the first results: pretraining copies, SFT contracts, DPO tilts. A little mathematics says why, and what it means for aligning models with what we want.

Further reading

Habits that travel

  • Model Misalignment and Language Change: Traces of AI-Associated Language in Unscripted Spoken English Anderson, Galpin and Juzek, AIES 2025 peer-reviewed Words associated with AI turning up in the way people speak, off the cuff. An association, not proof of a cause.
  • How LLMs Distort Our Written Language Abdulhai, White, Wan, Qureshi, Leibo, Kleiman-Weiner and Jaques, arXiv 2603.18161, 2026 preprint Essays largely written with a model took fewer sides; even grammar-only edits moved what the essays meant.

The em dash and other tells

  • Why Does ChatGPT “Delve” So Much? Exploring the Sources of Lexical Overrepresentation in Large Language Models Juzek and Ward, COLING 2025 peer-reviewed Where AI’s favourite words come from, and a first test of the role of human feedback.
  • ChatGPT and Gemini AIs Have Uniquely Different Writing Styles Karolina Rudnicka, Scientific American, 9 July 2025 journalism Each chatbot writes in a style of its own, much as each of us does. The paper behind it: Rudnicka and Juzek, “Beyond ‘AI Language’: The case for the idiolectal nature of LLM output”, arXiv 2608.06589 preprint
  • The Last Fingerprint: How Markdown Training Shapes LLM Prose E. M. Freeburg, arXiv 2603.27006, 2026 preprint The proposal that the em dash is markdown leaking into prose: one of the factors the dose grid is built to test.

The machinery

  • Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model Is Secretly a Reward Model Rafailov, Sharma, Mitchell, Ermon, Manning and Finn, NeurIPS 2023 peer-reviewed The preference step in the talk, and the tilted optimum behind “tilt”.
  • From Lists to Emojis: How Format Bias Affects Model Alignment Zhang, Xiong, Chen, Zhou, Huang and Zhang, ACL 2025 peer-reviewed Reward models favour lists, bold text and emojis; a small share of biased preference data is enough to skew them.
  • 2 OLMo 2 Furious Team OLMo et al., arXiv 2501.00656, 2024 preprint A fully open model, data included, stage by stage: the “sighting in the wild” in the talk. Its post-training recipe: Lambert et al., “Tülu 3: Pushing Frontiers in Open Language Model Post-Training”, arXiv 2411.15124 preprint

The dose grid, the ε/β pilots and the OLMo counts are our own work in preparation; the slides mark them as such. Every number in the talk, with its source, is in the slides’ appendix.

About the speaker

Tommie Juzek is Assistant Professor of Computational Linguistics at Florida State University. He works on why language models write the way they do, and what that is doing to human writing. More at tjuzek.com.
