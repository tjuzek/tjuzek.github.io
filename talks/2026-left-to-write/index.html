Richter’s Grey, the Machine’s Em Dash — and What Makes Us Us

About the talk

When Gerhard Richter painted his canvases grey, it was out of a crisis: he did not know what was left to paint. Many in the academy know the feeling now. In creative writing, in programming, in mathematical proof, machines pass the tests of whether a made thing works. What few of those tests were built to see is form.

This talk follows one piece of form, the em dash, from Dickinson to the Commonwealth Short Story Prize: how models came to overuse it, then partly stopped, and what training does and does not explain. What remains is the question: what makes us us when the machine writes well?

How machines write

  • ChatGPT and Gemini AIs Have Uniquely Different Writing Styles Karolina Rudnicka, Scientific American, 9 July 2025 journalism Each chatbot writes in a style of its own, much as each of us does. The paper behind it: Rudnicka and Juzek, “Beyond ‘AI Language’: The case for the idiolectal nature of LLM output”, arXiv 2608.06589 preprint
  • Model Misalignment and Language Change: Traces of AI-Associated Language in Unscripted Spoken English Anderson, Galpin and Juzek, AIES 2025 peer-reviewed Words associated with AI turning up in the way people speak, off the cuff. An association, not proof of a cause.
  • How LLMs Distort Our Written Language Abdulhai, White, Wan, Qureshi, Leibo, Kleiman-Weiner and Jaques, arXiv 2603.18161, 2026 preprint In one of its experiments, essays largely written by the model took fewer sides than essays written without it.

About the speaker

Tommie Juzek is Assistant Professor of Computational Linguistics at Florida State University. He works on why language models write the way they do, and what that is doing to human writing. More at tjuzek.com.
