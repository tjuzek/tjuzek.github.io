The theoretically informed and critically delimited conceptual point of departure consists in a carefully operationalisable diachronic hypothesis concerning the potentially consequential, historically mediated diffusion of model-preferred lexical choices into human-authored news discourse following the public dissemination of conversational generative artificial intelligence.

The central, empirically falsifiable and conceptually consequential research question is whether lexical items systematically and disproportionately favoured by contemporary language models exhibit a statistically and substantively noteworthy, potentially anomalous increase in the journalistic record after ChatGPT’s introduction, and whether any such increase manifests itself across a carefully differentiated, typologically heterogeneous, and geographically diverse set of languages.

Previous, methodologically distinct empirical investigations of scientific English have documented unusually pronounced and historically conspicuous increases, beginning around 2022, in the frequencies of particular lexical items associated with language-model-generated prose, including delve, underscore, and intricate, as exemplified by Kobak et al. (2025); nevertheless, the strikingly disproportionate concentration of this literature on English-language scientific writing leaves critically unresolved the generalisability question of whether the phenomenon extends beyond a single language and a single, unusually AI-exposed communicative domain.

This question possesses considerable theoretical and socio-linguistic significance beyond the relatively narrow task of lexical frequency description, because the mere-exposure framework associated with Zajonc (1968) offers an independently motivated, theoretically informed reason to investigate whether repeated encounters with linguistic forms may shape subsequent human preferences, while the empirical work of Abdulhai et al. (2026) provides a complementary and empirically substantiated motivation for examining the possibility that AI-mediated writing assistance can alter not only the surface composition but also the substantive communicative tendencies of the texts people produce; neither consideration, however suggestive, independently establishes the causal mechanism at issue in the present observational study.

H0Under the cautiously formulated and explicitly comparative null hypothesis, H0, the quantitatively ascertained diachronic change in the aggregate prevalence of the lexical items identified as disproportionately associated with AI generation is not systematically greater than the corresponding change observed for a carefully selected and methodologically defensible set of baseline lexical items. H1Under the substantively contrasting and empirically testable alternative hypothesis, H1, the post-2022 journalistic prevalence of AI-associated lexical items increases, relative to its earlier level, by a greater amount than the prevalence of the comparison or baseline items, thereby yielding an independently quantifiable, analytically interpretable divergence that is consistent with, although crucially not causally or uniquely attributable to, the diffusion of AI-preferred lexical patterns.

The first, foundationally important methodological phase systematically establishes a reproducibly and computationally derived, language-specific inventory of lexical items whose observed production frequencies measurably and nontrivially diverge between language-model continuations and corresponding human-authored journalistic material.

Rather than uncritically relying on a subjectively assembled, intuitively appealing, or manually curated catalogue of allegedly characteristic AI expressions, the investigators rigorously operationalise the otherwise potentially elusive construct of AI-associated lexicality through a systematically replicable computational comparison involving news texts in 34 languages, thereby identifying model-preferred items separately within each language-specific corpus.

To render the otherwise technically intricate continuation-based identification procedure pedagogically transparent, consider the following deliberately simplified, expressly illustrative, and wholly invented news sentence, initially encountered as an incomplete excerpt from an entirely hypothetical, historically situated 2020–21 journalistic report.The journalist’s intentionally fictitious and contextually minimal opening clause reads, “The new bridge over the river opened on Monday,” at which point the sentence is interrupted and the crucially relevant remaining content is systematically withheld from the model: The new bridge over the river opened on Monday,|…

A language model, GPT-4.1-mini, is then explicitly tasked with independently generating the continuation of the truncated passage, thereby producing a synthetically produced completion whose empirically observable lexical distribution can be contrasted with the previously withheld, genuinely human-authored continuation produced by the journalist.In this carefully delimited, entirely invented pedagogical demonstration, the model-generated continuation reads, “… and the mayor said it would revitalize the waterfront and emphasize the city’s commitment to its residents,” with the visually foregrounded lexical items serving as deliberately illustrative examples of the sorts of expressions whose relative production frequencies may prove quantitatively and diagnostically informative.

The methodologically decisive and carefully controlled comparison is not between two competing model outputs but between the algorithmically generated synthetic completion and the sentence’s genuinely authentic, previously concealed journalistic continuation, as originally written by the human journalist.In this deliberately stylised and strictly illustrative example, the journalist’s actual continuation instead reads, “… two years late, after the cost had doubled,” illustrating that the human-produced text may employ substantively different lexical, informational, and discourse-pragmatic choices from those preferred by the generative model.

The bridge sentence and both continuations are entirely fictional, expressly pedagogical inventions created for this slide and are not quotations or sampled observations from the underlying study; the study’s genuinely implemented, computationally systematic identification procedure drew upon up to 100,000 real news sentences per language.

The carefully operationalised analytical category designated AI words therefore consists of lexical items appearing systematically and substantially more frequently in the model’s generated continuations than in the journalists’ authentic continuations, with the 20 most disproportionately represented, empirically ranked items determined independently for each of the 34 languages rather than imposed through a common English-language vocabulary; for English, the 10 highest-ranking items reported are additionally, emphasize, revolutionize, revitalize, captivated, conservationist, streamline, firsthand, personalize, and introspective, a list whose relevance derives from the directly measured and quantitatively consequential model–human disparity rather than from any assertion that the words are intrinsically artificial or absent from ordinary human writing.

The second, analytically indispensable methodological phase carefully transforms the computationally established word inventories into a large-scale, temporally structured longitudinal corpus comparison, allowing the investigators to quantitatively and critically evaluate whether the prevalence of these items changed across the temporal discontinuity associated with the introduction of ChatGPT.

The unusually extensive and explicitly delimited empirical basis is the WMT News Crawl collection of web-based news writing, from which the study draws material in 34 languages and reports a data scale of 7.1 billion tokens; the comparison centres on the pre-ChatGPT period 2020–21 and the post-ChatGPT period 2023–24, with the public release of ChatGPT on 30 November 2022 supplying a historically salient and interpretatively useful chronological reference point, but emphatically not constituting a randomised or otherwise causally identifying treatment intervention.

The deliberately simplified but analytically informative visual chronology clearly distinguishes the earlier observational interval, labelled BEFORE: 2020–21, from the later interval, labelled AFTER: 2023–24, and precisely locates the public release of ChatGPT on 30 Nov 2022 between them.

The principal, explicitly operationalised quantitative outcome measure is a carefully normalised and cross-corpus comparable estimate of the relative prevalence of language-specific AI-associated lexical items in the news corpus.

More specifically, and in a methodologically transparent fashion, the investigators systematically calculate the proportion of all tokens accounted for by the 20 AI-associated words in the earlier and later periods and explicitly express the resulting temporal difference as a percentage change relative to the earlier period, thereby facilitating defensible, scale-adjusted comparisons across corpora whose absolute sizes are not identical.

The indispensable methodological comparison or control condition is supplied by a symmetrically specified, correspondingly sized lexical inventory of baseline lexical items selected for carefully assessed approximate agreement between model-generated and journalist-authored continuation frequencies.

Precisely the same token-normalised, compositionally comparable, and temporally contrasted calculation is repeated for 20 baseline words, which the model and the journalists had used at approximately equivalent rates, so that broader, potentially confounding fluctuations in word frequencies can be compared with the specifically hypothesised and quantitatively differential behaviour of the AI-associated inventory.

As a conceptually illuminating yet methodologically distinct supplementary observation, the source article identifies a second, theoretically intriguing phenomenon that is distinct from, although conceptually complementary to, the diachronic frequency analysis: the recurrence of strikingly analogous, model-preferred semantic categories across independently constructed language-specific word inventories.

Computationally and independently identified AI-associated word lists exhibit systematic and semantically meaningful cross-linguistic convergence in lexical meaning, suggesting that some model-associated preferences recur at a more abstract, conceptually interpretable level rather than being confined to individual English expressions.

Because the AI-associated word inventories were carefully generated independently for each language, the subsequent recurrence of particular semantic families cannot straightforwardly or satisfactorily be explained as the mechanical transplantation of one English word list into multiple corpora; notably, expressions belonging to the semantic domain of emphasize occur in the identified inventories of 24 of the 34 languages, providing an especially striking and theoretically provocative example of cross-lingual convergence that warrants careful, independently articulated theoretical explanation.

A critically informed, methodologically sophisticated, and epistemically nuanced appraisal must carefully and explicitly differentiate the genuine strengths of the article’s comparative operationalisation from the consequential epistemic and causal-inferential limits of observational temporal associations and must then translate those limits into a precisely operationalisable and empirically answerable research programme for further research.

The principal, independently appreciable methodological strength concerns the use of an explicitly and carefully constructed, theoretically justified comparison condition, rather than the interpretation of AI-associated word frequencies in isolation.

The deliberate and methodologically nuanced integration of a matched baseline lexical inventory within a multilingual, temporally aligned corpus design materially strengthens the inferential interpretability of the observed diachronic differences.

Because the baseline words and AI-associated words are consistently and transparently quantified by precisely the same procedure within the same journalistic materials, the comparison offers an important, albeit necessarily partial, methodological safeguard against the mistaken attribution of broad corpus-level lexical fluctuations to AI-associated vocabulary specifically; moreover, the use of 34 languages and separately identified lexical inventories reduces the dependence of the carefully circumscribed overall conclusion on the idiosyncrasies of English-language word choice, even though these design strengths do not eliminate the full range of potentially consequential confounding influences.

The central, critically important inferential weakness resides in the distinction between merely observing an ostensibly suggestive temporal association and rigorously and causally demonstrating that a particular historical intervention caused it.

The post-2022 changes are fundamentally observational and correlational findings and do not, without substantially stronger causal-identification strategies, amount to a methodologically persuasive demonstration of causally AI-induced effects.

Although the temporal alignment between the diffusion of generative AI and the subsequent increased prevalence of many model-associated lexical items is undeniably suggestive, it remains epistemically insufficient as a basis for causal inference, and the article appropriately does not claim to establish that such increases were caused by AI-assisted composition rather than by potentially consequential concurrent changes in news topics, editorial conventions, source composition, or other unmeasured determinants of lexical frequency; importantly, the article itself explicitly and responsibly acknowledges this fundamental restriction on causal interpretation.

The most intellectually productive and experimentally tractable follow-up question concerns the underlying computational and developmental origin of the model-side lexical preferences upon which the diachronic analysis is predicated.

Which specific, interacting pretraining and post-training mechanisms give rise to the systematic, recurrent, and disproportionately amplified overproduction of particular lexical items, such as emphasize, across contemporary language models?

A carefully preregistered and methodologically controlled subsequent study could seek to discriminate between several theoretically plausible but empirically distinguishable competing explanations – including imbalances in the pretraining distribution, model-internal generalisation, and preference amplification during instruction tuning or other post-training procedures – by comparing lexical propensities across model families and training stages under strictly standardised and carefully controlled elicitation conditions; such a programme would address the deeper generative origins of the empirically observed model behaviour rather than merely documenting the subsequent behaviour of news corpora.

My selection of this article is motivated by a broader, critically informed and epistemologically consequential theoretical concern with the potentially systematic discrepancy between the linguistic propensities acquired by contemporary generative systems and the communicative expectations, practices, and preferences that characterise situated, socially embedded human language use.

I find the research problem both intellectually consequential and increasingly socio-technically urgent because the behavioural distributions manifested by language models are not necessarily distributionally or pragmatically congruent with those of human writers or with the normative expectations that people bring to communication, yet our information environment increasingly contains texts produced directly by such systems or modified through their assistance; under these circumstances, even ostensibly minor, comparatively subtle, and individually inconspicuous lexical preferences may become socially consequential if their repeated circulation influences subsequent human expression, and the careful, empirically rigorous, and theoretically nuanced diagnosis of these potential influences constitutes a methodologically indispensable and ethically consequential preliminary step towards developing systems whose linguistic behaviour is better aligned with human communicative aims.

What critically examining, carefully synthesising, and systematically working through this material ultimately changed for me was the scale at which I understood the phenomenon: rather than treating the emergence of distinctive, systematically recurrent AI-associated phrasing as a localised curiosity of a particular scholarly register, I came to appreciate how linguistically heterogeneous, geographically extensive, and widely distributed its potential manifestations could be across linguistic communities and genres, and how the year 2022, while not an experimentally isolated causal intervention, provides a particularly salient, albeit methodologically imperfect, historical demarcation between earlier and later conditions of AI-mediated textual production.

This carefully contextualised and empirically complementary supplementary example, drawn from Abdulhai et al. (2026), provides an analytically distinct, more directly participant-centred, and theoretically complementary perspective on the possibility that access to language-model assistance may meaningfully influence substantive argumentative and semantic features of human-written discourse.

To experimentally interrogate potentially consequential shifts in argumentative stance, participants were asked to respond in essay form to the deceptively straightforward yet philosophically and interpretatively open-ended question, “Does money lead to happiness?”

Approximately 100 individually recruited adults in the United States each composed an essay on this topic, after which GPT-4o was used to systematically classify the expressed argumentative stance expressed in each essay, thus converting an inherently interpretative and otherwise qualitative argumentative feature into categories that could be compared across conditions of systematically differing degrees of AI involvement.

The explicitly operationalised stance categories carefully distinguished support for the proposition, a neutral position that took no side, and opposition to the proposition; the analytically separate writing conditions distinguished participants who wrote alone without AI, participants who used AI comparatively lightly for advice or look-ups, and participants who allowed AI to write much of the essay.

The substantively important and empirically most readily interpretable contrast is that approximately two in five essays written without AI adopted a comparatively noncommittal, explicitly neutral argumentative position, whereas approximately two in three essays in the condition involving extensive AI-generated writing adopted that position, a difference which raises the theoretically important but causally qualified possibility that the degree of generative-model involvement can be associated with a measurable and systematic redistribution of expressed argumentative stances.

A separate, critically informative experiment further indicated that models instructed ostensibly merely to undertake surface-level grammatical correction could nevertheless change the meaning of a text, thereby critically problematising the intuitively attractive yet empirically questionable assumption that seemingly surface-level language assistance is semantically and pragmatically neutral with respect to the semantic and pragmatic content of the resulting communication.
