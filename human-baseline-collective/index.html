Human Baseline Collective

Computational linguistics papers — discussed by humans.

Here is what Tommie has been thinking about a research podcast run by a small collective of researchers — so far Tommie Juzek and Matt Patience (Florida State University) and Yuri Bizzoni (Aarhus University). An early draft for discussion, for anyone joining or thinking about it; started 25 October 2026, revised 30 October 2026.

All of this is open — nothing is set. Whoever joins can give full input and change direction on anything.

AI disclaimer. The content of this write-up was co-developed by Tommie Juzek and an AI assistant (Claude, Anthropic). The assistant drafted the text upon Tommie Juzek's prompting; Tommie Juzek reviewed and approved it.

The proposal at a glance

  • Name: Human Baseline Collective — the human row in every benchmark table, kept by a group.
  • Idea: one paper per episode, rotating: papers by members of the collective, and an outside paper the hosts like.
  • Mix: at first, mostly the hosts' own papers and their students' — less work, and the authors are in the room. Say so openly in the show description. For the outside slot, invite the author when you can — guests bring listeners. Review the mix after season one.
  • Rhythm: every other month, seasons of six — pilot first, launch in early 2027.
  • Length: 20 to 40 minutes — stop when done, no padding.
  • Formats: audio feed plus full video on YouTube; clips later.
  • Cost: about 250 to 600 USD of kit per host; free software to start; for a guest without kit, a starter set under 100 USD, posted to keep.
  • Time: roughly half a day each per episode — a guess, not a measurement. That holds once you are in a rhythm; expect more for the first few episodes and for whoever edits. Knowing the paper cuts preparation, not recording, editing, or publishing. The test run logs hours per task.

Schedule and platforms — Tommie's proposal

  • Test run: Thursday 19 November 2026, 11:00 Tallahassee / 17:00 Aarhus — not published unless it turns out well. Judge it on: did you enjoy it, how many hours did each step take, does it still sound fine a week later, would you send it to a colleague?
  • Releases: the second Tuesday of every odd month, 9:00 / 15:00, scheduled in advance — 12 January, 9 March, 11 May, 13 July, 14 September, 9 November 2027.
  • Recording: about three weeks before each release, 11:00 / 17:00 — for example the third Thursday of even months (17 December; then 18 February, 15 April, 17 June, 19 August, 21 October). The weekday is open: any day none of the hosts teaches.
  • Platforms, all free: Spotify for Creators hosts the feed; one submission each puts it on Apple Podcasts and Amazon Music, and most other apps follow. Video on YouTube, transcripts on a show page, optionally a citable DOI per episode via Zenodo.
  • Before launch: a short trailer in December, so the show is already listed on Apple by 12 January. Kit only after the test run — and only if it is a go.

Why a podcast

  • People read the papers they care most about; the ones they are only somewhat interested in, they increasingly hear as AI-generated podcasts.
  • Those listeners could hear your papers from you — not from a synthetic host — with what only humans add: the backstory, what did not work, honest disagreement, and the authors as guests. In one line: everything an AI podcast says is in the PDF — you talk about what isn't.
  • Who it is for: anyone who would otherwise put the paper into NotebookLM — researchers and students in and around the field. So: assume the field, explain the methods. And since these listeners start from the paper, not from a podcast app, the episode link goes wherever the paper is: publication pages, the code README, a QR code on posters and slides, the post announcing the paper.
  • The gap: a quick search — not a survey — found no active podcast that discusses computational linguistics papers with their authors. The closest, AI2's NLP Highlights, has been on hiatus since 2024; Talking Papers discusses papers with their authors, but in vision and machine learning; The Gradient runs long interviews with researchers. Human Baseline Collective would be shorter, one paper at a time, with hosts who are often the authors, and about language and literature. The real competitor is the AI podcast itself.

What it could bring

  • Funding: a running series makes the dissemination part of a grant proposal believable — promising a podcast from scratch does not. NSF reviewers ask whether the team is qualified and how success will be assessed; Horizon Europe asks for communication and dissemination. Hence a simple log from episode 1.
  • Discoverability: published transcripts are read by search engines and LLMs.
  • Network: outside-paper authors as guests; guests share their episodes.
  • Students: co-authors on air; later, a student assistant for editing.
  • Each other: a structured conversation every two months.
  • Teaching: episodes as listening assignments.

A possible episode (about 30 minutes)

  1. Hook (1 min): the finding in one sentence.
  2. Why this paper (3 to 5): where it sits in the field.
  3. The paper (10 to 15): what was done and found; what is shown, and what is not.
  4. Not in the paper (8 to 10): what only the authors can tell — dead ends, a reviewer's comment, the figure that was cut, what is next.
  5. Close (1).

The AI version, now and then: an earlier draft had a fixed “AI version” segment (a minute of an AI podcast on the paper, and what it got wrong). It works better as an occasional special: as a fixed segment it costs time every episode, the terms for reusing the AI audio are unclear, it invites hunting for errors, and the name already makes the comparison. Alternatively, try it once in the test run and decide then.

Now and then: classics on anniversaries (“Attention Is All You Need” turns 10 in June 2027), one paper through two lenses (literary, and AI and language), students’ first papers, conference specials, an AI podcast on a paper set side by side with yours. Firth’s “company it keeps” synopsis turns 70 in 2027, too.

Starter kit

Hardware, per host

  • Dynamic USB/XLR mic: sound first
  • Boom arm, wired headphones
  • LED light: ALTSON clip-on (about 14 USD), or a SENSYNE ring light whose stand also holds the phone (about 30)
  • Phone at eye level as camera
  • Real background with depth

Software, free route

  • Talk over Jitsi or Zoom; each host records locally (OBS)
  • Edit: Kdenlive, Audacity
  • Transcripts, captions: Whisper
  • Feed: Spotify for Creators; video: YouTube
  • Show page with transcripts: GitHub Pages
  • Paid tools (Riverside, Descript; about 40 to 70 USD a month) only if editing time hurts

Guests: they do not need host kit — wired earbuds and a quiet room are the minimum. For a guest who has nothing, Tommie's idea is a starter set under 100 USD, ordered on Amazon and posted to them to keep: a FIFINE AM8T dynamic USB mic with its boom arm (about 69 USD; earbuds plug straight into the mic) and wired earbuds (about 10). At six episodes a year, that is 600 USD at most, usually less. Ordered from the guest's local Amazon store; who pays is open. For the recording, Zoom keeps a separate track per person, and the guest records a backup on their phone.

Keeping it light

One template for every episode · light edits only · one episode in the bank · one owner per task · seasons — so stopping is a choice, not a fade · universities share the episodes · a student assistant once the routine is stable.

Help to ask for

  • FSU media and Arts and Sciences: sharing episodes first; then a setup check and a studio for in-person specials.
  • Aarhus: the Center for Advanced Studies has podcast staff; the Interacting Minds Centre runs a podcast.
  • An independent show — the members' universities credited, none branding it.

What it is — and is not

  • A conversation, not a lecture.
  • One paper with its authors, not a career interview.
  • Papers the hosts like — never takedowns.
  • Pre-recorded and edited, never live.
  • A side project, not a second job.
  • Transcribed, not just recorded.

Over to you

Everything above is a starting point, not a set agenda. Tommie would value your view on anything — the rhythm, video, who does what, the AI segment, the mix of papers, the guest kit, or a different direction altogether. Once the collective agrees, possible next steps: pick the first season's papers, set a pilot date, get the kit, ask the communications offices, and set up hosting and the log.
