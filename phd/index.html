Doing a PhD with me

Language models have developed their own ways of using language, and those habits are now meeting ours.

If you want to find out where these habits come from, and what they mean for human language, a PhD in computational linguistics with me could be for you. This page answers the questions I am asked most often, so that you can judge the fit for yourself.

In short (as of September 2026)

  • Fall 2027: I am open to supervising a PhD student with a strong computational linguistics profile.
  • Funding: I do not have research assistant (RA) funding. Funding would come through the programme, is merit-based, and typically takes the form of a teaching assistantship.
  • Fit: in my group, language is the object of study. Your CV should show computational work on language.
  • Route: the PhD in Scientific Computing at Florida State University. To be considered for funding, apply by 15 January 2027.
  • Eligibility: US federal rules and Florida state rules currently restrict some applicants; please check below.
  • Before you write: please read If you write.

Already at FSU? You do not need to go through this page: see working with students and reach out directly.

What you could work on

Much of my current research starts from a simple observation: language models have recognisable ways of using language, and those patterns increasingly interact with human language use. That opens up questions a PhD could take on, for example:

  • Where do model language preferences come from? Pre-training, instruction tuning, and learning from human feedback each leave traces, and telling them apart is an open problem.
  • How do these preferences travel into human writing and speech, across registers and across languages?
  • How can we measure all of this reliably, without hand-picked word lists?
  • What does it mean for how we think about language change, when part of the language humans read and learn from is machine-generated?

These are examples, not a fixed menu. Students in my group have co-authored peer-reviewed papers on questions like these (see my research), we share tools and data openly, such as the AI Word Explorer, and the work has drawn wide public interest.

What I look for

In my group, language is the object of study, not only the input to a model. I am looking for two things:

  • Your computational language work so far: projects where the findings are about language, for instance about words, grammar, meaning, discourse, variation, or change. Your CV should show this.
  • Your computational language questions going forward: what you would like to find out about language, in your own words.

Strong skills in machine learning or LLM engineering are valuable, and there is often overlap in methods. On their own, however, they are not a fit. If your work centres on computer vision, multimodal systems, model efficiency, or general machine-learning evaluation, you will likely find better-matched, and often better-funded, positions elsewhere. That is no judgement on the quality of your work.

A note on emails

Many of the emails I receive follow the same pattern: a careful summary of my most recent paper, followed by a paragraph connecting it to the sender’s own work. I appreciate the effort, but such emails tell me little. Summaries like that have become easy to produce, and they describe my work rather than yours. Please skip the summary. What I would like to know is what you have done with language, and what you want to find out about it.

If you write

Please first judge the fit for yourself, using the points above. If you see a strong fit, email me at tjuzek@fsu.edu with:

  • the subject line PhD Fall 2027: Your Name;
  • a few sentences on your computational language work so far, with links where possible;
  • a few sentences on the language questions you would like to pursue;
  • your CV (PDF);
  • any specific question that this page does not answer.

I read every email, but I may not be able to reply to messages that this page already answers.

How to apply

The route is the PhD in Scientific Computing at Florida State University, with a research focus on computational linguistics. Admission decisions are made by the department, not by me.

  • Deadline for funding: complete applications received by 15 January 2027 are considered for the department’s assistantships and for university fellowships. Later deadlines exist, but only for any remaining funding. See the department’s application page.
  • Requirements, such as English-language tests: please check the application page and the department’s FAQ, as details change from time to time.
  • Advisor: the programme expects students to find a faculty member who agrees, informally, to serve as their advisor. If you fit the profile above, that is the point at which writing to me makes sense.

Eligibility, visas, and Florida rules

This is a common question, and the rules have changed several times recently. I cannot advise on individual cases; the official sources below are authoritative. As of September 2026:

  • FSU: the Graduate School’s international admissions page and the Center for Global Engagement’s immigration updates set out the university’s position, including English-language requirements and visa documents.
  • US federal rules: a presidential proclamation in force since 1 January 2026 suspends new student visas (F and J) for nationals of a number of countries; NAFSA keeps a summary with the list of countries. FSU’s international admissions page notes that visas will not be issued to affected applicants while the restriction is in place.
  • Florida state rules: under section 288.860 of the Florida Statutes, as applied by the Board of Governors and by FSU, the university needs additional approval before employing a graduate assistant who is domiciled in one of seven “countries of concern”: China (including Hong Kong and Macau), Cuba, Iran, North Korea, Russia, Syria, and Venezuela. See FSU’s guidance. As assistantships are the main source of PhD funding, please check this early if it applies to you.
